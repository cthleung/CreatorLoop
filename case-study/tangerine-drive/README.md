# The tangerine drive

This is a single scene from a novel I've been writing, traced from the first idea to the published version, with every stage kept on record. If you want to see how I actually work with an AI on creative writing (who decides what, and where the machine falls flat on its face), this is probably the best way in.

The scene itself is short, and it's pretty simple. 2 bandmates in their early 20s are driving up a foggy mountain road on Jeju Island, on a supply run for the reality show they're filming, with a box of tangerines in the passenger seat and a 1972 trot song on loop. They bicker, they play a game with their eyes closed, and somewhere near the top, one of them asks the other if they should build a house up here someday. It opens a chapter, and it doesn't pay off until much later in the novel (which is part of why I really cared about getting it right).

## Where it came from

It started with a song. I'd been digging through the reference footage I build these characters from, and found a moment where one of them sings a line from Nam Jin's "님과 함께" ("With You"), an old trot classic about building a house like a painting on a green meadow and living there with the one you love. My first reaction was "This is brilliant." My very next message, though, was "Don't draft yet, I want to do the microblocking of the scene myself."

That line is, more or less, how I use AI for writing in general. The idea's mine, and the shape of the scene is too. The machine helps me check and draft, but it doesn't get to decide what the scene's about.

## The outline

My outline ([01-outline.md](01-outline.md)) is a single rambling message, written like I'd pitch a scene to a friend over drinks - open on the song, then the U-turn joke, then the eyes-closed smelling game, then the house line, and then a fade-out on her drifting thoughts, "Tangerines. Ocean. Rock. Ocean. Tangerines." It's rough, and I said so in the message itself, because a lot of it leaned on footage details I only sort of remembered. So before anyone wrote a word of prose, everything got fact checked.

## The fact check

The AI went through the footage and the research notes claim by claim ([02-fact-check.md](02-fact-check.md)), and a few of my guesses turned out to be wrong in really useful ways. I had the scene happening right after a tangerine-picking trip, but the show was filmed in March, after the field tangerines are done, so the orchard became "last time" and a box of hallabong went in the car instead. I also had the girl answering the house question with a grunt. Then I went back and watched the real scene myself, and her actual reply was "That'd be nice." Which, well, is SO much better than anything I'd have come up with, so it became the ending.

The rule for using any of this is pretty simple. A real detail goes in as a prop or a habit, 1 clause, and the narration never explains it. If you know the footage, you'll catch it. If you don't, you just get a scene.

## The plan, and the options

Next, the AI wrote a scene plan ([03-scene-plan.md](03-scene-plan.md)), and here's where the most important habit in my process shows up. Wherever a line could go several ways, I just ask for options, lettered (a), (b) and (c), with the AI's pick first. Then I choose 1, or combine 2, or throw them all out and write my own.

For the opening line I threw them all out, because I wanted the scene to start on a black frame (dialogue in the dark, no tags, the names inside the lines telling you who's talking), which none of the options did, so that's that. For the next few lines, I actually took (a) every time. That probably sounds like a small thing, but every pick is a tiny recorded judgment about my taste, and a project like mine piles up thousands of them, which might be the most useful data I've ever produced without meaning to. I also did the microblocking pass with it, which is all the little physical stuff that makes a car ride feel real (the eggs rattling on the hairpins, the indicator ticking for nobody on an empty road, the house she draws in the fogged-up window), and took every idea.

## Draft 1

Then there's the first draft ([04-draft-1.md](04-draft-1.md)), which the AI wrote and reviewed itself. Its own scanner found 0 hard problems and 6 soft ones, and it'd dismissed every soft one with a reason. On paper it was clean.

That being said, it was actually kind of dead on the page, and that's the part no scanner's ever going to catch. The girl telling the story barely had a voice. There were maybe 2 lines of her own thoughts in the draft, and the rest read like a neutral narrator describing a nice car ride. Worse, she kept calling her bandmate "unnie" - the respectful word for an older sister - all the way through, which she never, ever does anywhere else in the novel.

## My review was 1 line

Looking back, this bit's the one I find most interesting. My review of draft 1 ([05-review-notes.md](05-review-notes.md)) came down to 1 line.

> Check for non-events and invented numbers, even figurative ones. Also needs way more FID, I think, and Seoyun's terminology.

(FID is free indirect discourse, when the narration slips into a character's own head and words.)

The AI turned that line into a full itemized sweep - 13 narrated non-events ("she said instead," "without taking her eyes off the road"), every invented number down to "check her mirror twice," and 13 specific spots where her inner voice belonged and wasn't. Then a few minutes later I added the bit no audit would've found on its own, that Seoyun never calls Haeun "unnie." Oh, and none of it got fixed yet. I'd asked for that on purpose ("change nothing for now"), so the notes went into the plan first and the next draft had a real checklist to work from.

That split, in my experience, is where the AI is most useful. I can usually name what's wrong in a sentence, because I know what the scene should feel like, which is the part I'd never hand off to anyone, let alone a machine. The AI can then go through every line and find every instance, which I'd frankly never have the patience for.

## 5 rounds of revision

The rest happened over the next day or so ([06-revisions.md](06-revisions.md)), and it got more and more hands-on as it went, to the point where I was selecting lines straight in the editor and asking for changes one at a time, because by then I knew what I wanted and it was faster to just point at it. A few moments from that stretch say more than I could.

- **The redraft quietly dropped the image the scene was built around** - both their faces in the car window, with her little fog drawing between them. I noticed it'd gone missing, asked where it went, and it came back as its own beat.
- **The AI added a music box to the dialogue,** and I asked straight out, "Where did the music box thing come from? Was it made up?" It wasn't, as it turns out (it's in the footage, along with the bill, down to the won), but I always check.
- **When the fixes started piling up, I pulled the other way:** "Smirk is fine. We can have some stock-face here and there, for variety and to avoid overwriting/overblocking everything." A style rule's there to catch reflexes, and I never wanted it boxing in every line.
- **My own designs beat the rules:** The ending breaks into short fragments, which my rules would normally flag, but linking the 2 scenes with that staccato was my idea in the first place, so it's staying.

## Side by side

On that note, here are a few spots where draft 1 and the final version differ, so you can see what the review actually bought.

**The opening:** Draft 1 opens on a tagged line, with the narrator setting the scene.

> "Seoddaeng-ah, how many times are you gonna play this?" Haeun asked, laughing, with her seat pulled so far forward that her chin was practically resting on the steering wheel. "It's been this one song the whole way up the mountain."

The final opens on the black frame I asked for.

> "Seoddaeng-ah, how many times are you gonna play this?"
>
> "Shh, Haeddeong, this is the best part -"
>
> "Hey, you've been saying that since the bottom of the mountain -"

**Her voice:** Draft 1, when she draws on the fogged-up window:

> While the song came back around to the start, Seoyun drew on her side of the glass with one finger, a little house with a pointy roof and lumpy tangerine trees all around it. When they stopped at a junction to let nobody past, Haeun glanced over at it, then back at the road.

The final, where she'd rather make a joke than admit anything:

> While the song started over, Seoyun drew on her side of the glass with a fingertip, a house with a pointy roof and tangerine trees all round it. The trees came out lumpy.
>
> Haeun glanced over. "Is that a house?"
>
> "It's a potato farm."


**A non-event:** Draft 1 tells you what she didn't do ("Seoyun's feet stayed where they were"). The final shows what she did: "Seoyun opened one eye and crossed her ankles on the dash."

**The ending:** Draft 1 has her answer the house question with "*Mm*," twice, and the final uses her real reply from the footage.

> "Is that a yes?"
>
> Seoyun kept her forehead on the glass. "That'd be nice."
>
> Haeun giggled. "Okay, I'm writing that down."

You'll find the finished scene in [07-final.md](07-final.md).

## What stayed behind

The scene's been published since, but the bigger outcome, honestly, is everything that outlived it. Several rulings from this scene alone went into my style guide, my scanner and its tests, so they apply to every scene I've written since: narrated non-events, invented numbers (figurative ones included), similes about how someone says a line ("casual as suggesting lunch," which I'd rather just replace with an adverb), bickering where nobody repeats the other's line back, and dialogue tags that follow what the narrator can actually perceive (she can't see a smirk with her eyes shut).

In other words, my taste gets carried forward, so the system's imprinted with it and I don't have to do as much refinement in the future. The next scene starts a little closer to what I want, and I spend less time saying the same thing twice. Which, as far as I'm concerned, is the entire reason I built any of this.
